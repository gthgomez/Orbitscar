# Edgeworld Archaeology

**Status:** RESEARCH BASELINE — not product authority
**Access date:** 2026-08-30
**Clean-room rule:** This document records mechanics as evidence only. No archived Edgeworld asset, text, map, name, audio, or extracted file is approved for reuse.

## Executive reconstruction

Edgeworld was a Kabam social strategy game released on Facebook in 2011, later associated with an iOS beta, and shut down in 2018 according to surviving third-party game-history records. The strongest primary-adjacent evidence is a 2011 producer interview describing the launch loop and the forthcoming alliance territory system; surviving wikis and player guides fill in later mechanics but are community-maintained and sometimes contradictory.

The design was closer to an asynchronous base-attack game with a visible battle resolution than to a conventional RTS. Players built a persistent base, scouted targets, selected and placed a limited army, watched a largely autonomous battle, then acted on the report. The strategic world layer made alliance territory contiguous after the first capture. This combination—not the specific science-fiction fiction—creates the useful design DNA.

## Reconstructed core loop

1. Start with a command-centered colony and a small set of economy/production structures.
2. Build and upgrade economy, military production, research, and defenses.
3. Select a target from PvE, PvP, or map activity.
4. Scout the target to inspect defenses and resources.
5. Choose a force and deploy units at legal points under a limited army/warp capacity.
6. Observe autonomous units navigate and attack; use available tactical interventions only if the ruleset provides them.
7. Resolve victory, retreat, destruction, loot, XP, repairs, and a battle report.
8. Improve the base or army based on the report.
9. Join an alliance and participate in territory capture, reinforcement, or diplomacy.

## Combat evidence

The contemporary Game Developer analysis explicitly describes the combat round as opponent selection, attack initiation, unit selection/placement, non-interactive combat, and outcome. A contemporary review emphasizes scouting, choosing a team, deployment location, and the effect of base layout. A player strategy guide records multiple warp opportunities, retreat, target analysis, defense coverage, and separate PvE/PvP considerations.

Observed design implications:

- Placement was meaningful because defensive range coverage and exposed resource structures created different attack plans.
- The battle was readable enough to watch but did not demand RTS-level continuous input.
- A deployment/warp constraint made pre-battle composition and timing consequential.
- Reports turned an asynchronous attack into a persistent social story.
- The mechanic could feel like agency without requiring synchronized opponents.

Unknown or weakly supported: exact unit AI, exact reinforcement timing, exact battle duration, exact target-priority rules, and the complete commander/ability roster.

## Base design archetypes

Surviving documentation supports these system categories: command center, resource extraction/refining, energy/power, storage or supply, barracks/staging, research/defense labs, warp/deployment, multiple turret families, and later special/utility structures. The evidence supports archetypes only; exact names and visual expression are explicitly rejected for reuse.

The design lesson is that placement needs a real tradeoff. If all buildings are disposable level gates, layout becomes decoration. If resources, production, sensors, and defenses expose different risk profiles, the base becomes a strategic instrument.

## World map evidence

Community documentation describes a 400×200 grid of player and NPC bases, alliance ownership, a first capture that could be placed freely, and subsequent captures constrained to adjacency. The 2011 producer interview separately describes a planet map where the home base and nearby hostile, allied, or neutral outposts form a contiguous expansion problem. Community documentation also describes recurring alliance rewards, capture level reduction, repair behavior, and a 10,000-base alliance cap; these later details are lower confidence.

Design lesson: adjacency creates fronts, logistics, and political geography. It also creates snowball and timezone risks, so a modern successor must add catch-up, safe entry, and anti-monopoly rules from the start.

## Progression and monetization evidence

The contemporary critique describes convoluted technology, duplicate production structures, long unit/build timers, and a progression pace perceived as pay-to-play. This is a useful warning, not a precise monetization ledger. Surviving community guides mention premium currency, warps, resource bottlenecks, upgrades, and rewards, but exact price tables are not used because they are historically contingent and potentially expressive of the original game.

The successor should preserve choice density while removing waiting as the primary business model.
